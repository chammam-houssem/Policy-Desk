Making Interdependence Visible: Information, Institutions, and the Water-Energy Nexus

How information flows and institutional rules shape adaptation across Tunisia's water and energy sectors.

Houssem Chammam Policy Researcher

TL;DR: Water and energy systems can be physically interdependent while their institutions remain disconnected. Comparing Tunisia's utilities shows how financial reporting can make resource pressures actionable, while technical self-reliance can keep them out of strategic planning.

How does water scarcity affect the energy transition? And how do water and energy utilities plan for an increasingly uncertain future?

These questions have important implications at a time when the impacts of climate change are becoming increasingly visible, and adaptation remains an open governance challenge. What forms of adaptation should we pursue? How should climate adaptation be governed? And how can institutions undertake long-term infrastructure investments when uncertainty is high and environmental pressures are cascading into each other?

These were some of the questions that motivated my recently published article with CIDOB, where I examined how water scarcity affects energy planning in Tunisia and how the institutional relationship between the water and energy sectors shapes adaptation.

What do we know?

Modern water systems are critically dependent on energy supply. Electricity is required for pumping, treatment, distribution, and especially for technologies designed to respond to water scarcity. In water-scarce regions, this dependence can grow further as governments turn toward desalination, deeper groundwater extraction, and long-distance water transfers, all of which increase the energy intensity of water provision.

The relationship also works in the opposite direction. Conventional fossil-fuel-based thermal electricity generation depends heavily on water, particularly for cooling and steam-based production. This can leave energy systems vulnerable to drought while also placing additional pressure on already scarce water resources.

For these reasons, water scarcity can also affect the incentives surrounding the energy transition. Renewable energy technologies with relatively low operational water needs, particularly wind and solar photovoltaic, can become more attractive where water availability is constrained. In that sense, water security and energy transition objectives can sometimes reinforce one another.

We therefore already know a great deal about the physical water-energy nexus: how much electricity water systems require, how much water energy technologies consume, and how scarcity can alter the relative attractiveness of infrastructure choices.

Yet, one of the earliest findings of my research was that climate and resource pressures do not automatically enter institutional decision-making simply because they are materially important.

Mapping the Institutional Landscape

The starting point for my research was a puzzling divergence between how Tunisia’s water sector has been adapting to intensifying energy security problems versus how the energy sector has been adapting to the intensifying water scarcity.

Tunisia’s national water utility, SONEDE, has extensive documentation of its electricity consumption, energy-efficiency measures, and plans for managing its energy dependence. STEG, Tunisia’s national electricity and gas utility, despite operating a predominantly gas-based thermal electricity system with significant water requirements, had no documentation of its water dependence.

I found no equivalent strategy for water use, no systematic reporting of water consumption, and almost no discussion or planning of how increasing water scarcity could affect electricity production. This is particularly striking in one of the world’s most water-stressed countries, where climate change is already placing significant pressure on water availability.

Why do severe, co-evolving resource scarcities fail to produce integrated cross-sectoral policy responses?

To investigate this question, I conducted interviews across Tunisia’s water and energy sectors, from operational engineers and regional managers to senior and executive leadership. I then used the Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) Framework, together with the Networks of Adjacent Action Situations (NAAS) approach, to reconstruct how decision-making processes are connected across the two systems.

Key Insight

Mapping institutional processes can reveal how decision spaces are connected, what information travels between them, and where that information becomes sufficiently salient and actionable to generate institutional change.

Rather than treating each utility as a single organization making a single set of decisions, the approach allowed me to map the different decision spaces through which outcomes are produced as the aggregate of multiple procedures and decision points: planning, financing, infrastructure investment, operations, reporting, oversight, and coordination.

Using the IAD-NAAS framework, I could trace the rules, incentives, and information flows connecting these processes and examine how they changed over time.

The resulting institutional maps reveal why biophysical vulnerabilities do not automatically trigger policy reform and institutional adaptation. In particular, their comparison shows that two sectors can be deeply interdependent without that interdependence becoming meaningful information for the organizations and people making strategic decisions.

The institutional map of Tunisia’s water-sector planning system provides a useful starting point for seeing how this works in practice.

Institutional Adaptation Depends on Information Infrastructure

Climate and resource pressures do not automatically enter institutional decision-making simply because they are materially important. Whether they shape institutional adaptation depends on whether those pressures are made visible, transmitted across decision spaces, and translated into actionable information.

Reporting obligations play an important role in this process. But information infrastructures can also emerge through everyday organizational processes, including budgeting, payments monitoring, operational responsibility, and oversight. In other words, institutional adaptation capacity depends partly on how information about external pressures enters and travels through a system, and on which actors are required to respond to its consequences.

As mapped in Figure 1: Water System Planning, SONEDE’s central planning situation (AS-WSP) is directly connected to energy tariff setting (AS 10), ministerial policy coordination (AS 4), and fiscal control (AS 5). Because rising electricity tariffs directly threaten SONEDE's balance sheet, fiscal oversight forced the utility to adjust information rules to mandate continuous energy auditing and metric harmonization.

In contrast, Figure 2: Energy System Planning illustrates a structural disconnect in STEG's planning logic. Water-for-energy operations (AS 11) exist merely as an isolated, second-degree adjacent situation linked only to operational management (AS 9). Central energy planning (AS-ESP) remains dominated by gas import negotiations (AS 8) and fiscal control (AS 10). Crucially, scope rules within AS-ESP restrict decision variables strictly to fuel supply logistics and grid stability, procedurally and legally excluding hydrological metrics from national energy models.

1. Why the Water Sector Internalized Energy Security Constraints

Cross-sectoral institutional connectivity is partly rule-governed. Reporting obligations, monitoring requirements, oversight procedures, and planning rules determine what information must move from one decision space to another and which problems eventually reach strategic decision-makers.

One of the most interesting findings of the research was that financial flows can generate information infrastructures that support institutional adaptation.

The relationship between SONEDE and STEG is partly market-based: SONEDE purchases electricity from STEG and must continuously account for those costs in its own operations.

Driven by energy-intensive activities like deep groundwater extraction and seawater desalination, electricity represents 30% of SONEDE’s total operating expenses (OPEX). The water utility’s institutional structure had to adapt to this pressure. Energy considerations became incorporated into the rules governing organizational responsibilities and expertise. Positions with responsibility for energy management were created, while technical knowledge of energy efficiency became increasingly relevant to planning and decision-making.

These financial flows also invite external oversight. Energy expenditures become relevant not only to SONEDE’s internal management, but also to the ministries and public authorities responsible for budgetary oversight. This creates additional pathways through which information about energy dependence can travel beyond operational concerns.

Over time, this repeated process of measurement, budgeting, and cost management has allowed SONEDE to develop increasingly granular knowledge of its own energy dependence. Therefore, because SONEDE relies entirely on electricity purchased from STEG, this fiscal interdependence institutionalized shared data exchange, lowered transaction and coordination costs, and eliminated cross-sectoral information asymmetries.

This is one reason why mapping institutional processes matters. It allows us to see how decision spaces are connected, what information moves between them, and where that information becomes sufficiently visible and actionable to generate institutional change.

2. Why the Energy Sector Decoupled from Water Scarcity

The energy sector presents almost the inverse situation. Water scarcity already affects electricity generation in Tunisia, but STEG has historically been able to manage many of these pressures at the operational level through technical adaptations, including desalination and direct seawater cooling.

For example, following severe droughts in 2001 and 2007 that threatened power operations, STEG used its large capital budget to invest in on-site seawater desalination at the Radès complex, and later for the rest of its thermal operations. By self-producing its water needs, STEG established an "outside option" that decoupled its operations from its institutional counterpart, SONEDE.

Because STEG extracts and treats seawater on-site without purchasing water from SONEDE, no financial transactions or utility bills take place. Water costs are absorbed as internal plant maintenance expenses, leaving STEG with zero external reporting obligations regarding water usage, a process of informational erasure. As a result, water consumption metrics are omitted from national water statistics and energy planning.

STEG treats water strictly as a localized engineering parameter managed at the plant level (AS 11). The utility's Directorate of Strategy and Planning completely excludes water availability, water metrics, and hydrological risks from its long-term energy expansion models (AS-ESP).

This explains why STEG does not maintain a figure for how much water its electricity system requires. The relevant information exists, but it remains fragmented among operational engineers and individual facilities rather than travelling systematically into higher-level energy planning.

However, this plant-level adaptation generates severe systemic trade-offs. Higher summer temperatures and rising seawater temperatures increase cooling pressures and the need for desalinated water. Yet the very capacity to resolve these problems locally has also limited their institutional visibility. When operational engineers can respond to a constraint themselves, and there is no corresponding requirement to aggregate and report that constraint upwards, it may never become a strategic planning variable.

Key Insight

Tunisia's power grid relies almost entirely on gas-fired thermal plants (95–97% of generation), requiring continuous water for cooling and steam cycles. Extreme summer heatwaves induce operational derating of 9.5%–12% of total grid capacity. At the Radès thermal complex (10% of national power), operational freshwater intensity surges from 0.029 m³/MWh in winter to 0.041 m³/MWh in summer (+41%), creating severe vulnerabilities during peak drought periods.

Systemic Consequences for the Energy Transition

This institutional decoupling creates a maladaptive feedback loop that hinders Tunisia's broader energy transition.

  1. Concealed Advantage of Renewables: Because water use in power generation is unpriced and unmetered in official levelized cost models (LCOE), the drought-resilience co-benefits of low-water renewable technologies are hidden in official planning. The erasure of this externality preserves the apparent economic advantage of gas thermal power.
  2. Reinforced Carbon Lock-in: Operating on-site seawater desalination consumes significant internal electricity (3.15 MWh daily at the Radès complex). At the same time, during summer heatwaves, extreme ambient air and water temperatures cause thermal derating across STEG's natural gas plants, resulting in a 9.5% to 12% loss in total generating capacity. However, because water is treated as an engineering parameter, the solution adopted by STEG to this structural challenge is to add more desalination units, exhibiting a classic case of technological path dependency.

Academic lessons: Institutional Climate Adaptation in the Water-Energy-Food-Ecosystem (WEFE) Nexus

The contrast between the water and energy sectors in Tunisia is productive.

SONEDE is repeatedly pushed to internalize its dependence on the energy system because that dependence appears throughout its financial and administrative processes through multiple pathways. The result is a dense information infrastructure, where repeated measurement and financial exposure gradually produce actionable knowledge.

When pressures to improve energy efficiency and accelerate the energy transition intensified, SONEDE already possessed information about how much energy it consumed, where reductions were possible, which technologies were available, and what investments would be required to implement them.

STEG, by contrast, has historically been able to manage many cross-sectoral pressures through decoupling. The extension of the energy mandate into water self-production was possible because energy in Tunisia is governed as a critical matter of national security, granting STEG significant institutional power, autonomy, and political weight. This has allowed strategic energy planning to remain comparatively insulated from the water system on which electricity production physically depends, hindering STEG’s capacity to detect and act upon this rising challenge.

Key Insight

As Jack Knight’s (1992) institutional bargaining theory suggests, actors with powerful outside options shape rule structures to insulate themselves from adjacent constraints. When fiscal coupling institutionalizes reporting interfaces, it builds enduring data infrastructures that lower coordination costs; conversely, technical decoupling creates informational erasure, insulating central decision-makers from cross-sectoral ecological constraints.

Policy lessons:

Across the literature on the water-energy nexus and the broader water-energy-food-ecosystem (WEFE) nexus, data gaps are consistently identified as the most critical barrier to effective nexus-based decision-making (Helerea et al., 2023). The omission of water-for-energy data leads to an incomplete and often misleading representation of the interlinkages between water and energy, ultimately weakening the quality of policy planning and investment decisions.

  • Inter-Agency Boundary Rules: Establish cross-sectoral oversight bodies with mandatory nexus impact assessments to prevent single-sector technical adaptations from destabilizing adjacent resource systems.
  • Mandatory Cross-Sectoral Reporting: Embed operational water intensity, thermal derating risks, and auxiliary cooling power requirements directly into power expansion modeling and Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE) algorithms.
  • Institutionalizing Shadow Pricing: Introduce shadow water valuation into power grid dispatch rules to highlight the comparative economic advantage of low-water renewable technologies such as wind and solar PV.

Re-architecting institutional information flows and rule configurations provides an essential roadmap for climate-resilient resource governance across the Mediterranean and broader MENA regions.
